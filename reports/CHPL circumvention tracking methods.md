# Open trackers can close the CHPL evidence gap

Researchers and governments track CHPL goods to Russia with four method families: exporter-reported HS6 trade with control groups (EBRD, ifo/Kiel, BOFIT), mirror-gap ("lost in transit") ratios (EBRD), Russian transaction-level customs records with brand and seller attribution (KSE, Kiel), and partner-reported CHPL-only trackers (KSE quarterly tracker, S&P, Carnegie). Their main results agree. Direct Western CHPL exports to Russia collapsed. A detour through Armenia, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan exists, but it replaced only a small part of the lost direct trade. China and Hong Kong now ship most CHPL goods to Russia. Detours through Central Asia became smaller after 2023, under secondary-sanctions pressure. All serious studies say that partner data and intra-EAEU data undercount the true flow. Our project measures a descriptive version of the EBRD design: two legs from two different reporters, with no control group. Our numbers agree with independent figures where a check is possible. For example, our Kazakhstan-to-Russia series rises about 390% in 2022 and falls about 4% in 2023, and a Comtrade-based press figure gives +401% and −5%. No public product combines CHPL detail, Central Asian corridors, open code and data, an interactive view and a regular update. The closest product, Burana / Corridor Watch, has the same corridors and list, plus a control country and a mirror gap, but it gives no data download. Our project can take the "open and reproducible" position. To get there, it must add a mirror check of the first leg, control goods and destinations, tier views, monthly EU data, explicit data-availability flags and open-data packaging.

## Four method families answer different questions

The literature uses four methods. Each method answers a different question. Each method has a different blind spot.

### Exporter-reported HS6 data with control groups (EBRD, ifo/Kiel, BOFIT)

The EBRD "Eurasian roundabout" paper is the reference design. It uses **monthly HS6 exporter-reported data from UN Comtrade, Jan 2017–Aug 2022**, plus China customs data to December 2022 ([EBRD WP 276](https://www.ebrd.com/content/dam/ebrd_dxp/assets/pdfs/office-of-the-chief-economist/working-papers/working-papers-2023/WP-276.pdf)). It does not use the CHPL. It maps the EU Regulation 833/2014 annexes and the EU dual-use list to **1,983 "sanctioned" HS6 codes**. It adds **1,452 "similar" codes** in the same HS4, to catch misclassification, and 1,974 "other" codes as a base group. The model is a triple difference: post-March 2022 × sanctioned product × importer group (Russia; Armenia, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan; Georgia). Azerbaijan, Mongolia and the rest of the world are comparators. The model also tests the extensive margin (0/1 trade), because more than half of monthly HS6 cells are zero. A "substitution ratio" divides the increase in exports to the Central Asian group by the fall in direct exports to Russia, per HS6 line (same source).

The ifo/Kiel paper by Scheckenhofer, Teti and Wanner uses annual Comtrade data and goods tied to the EU CHP list. It runs two triple differences on the extensive margin, with pair×product, exporter×product×year and importer×product×year fixed effects. These fixed effects separate normal trade diversion from evasion. The paper also runs a re-export test, but only three countries report re-exports in Comtrade: Georgia, Moldova and Uzbekistan ([Scheckenhofer et al. 2025](https://feodorateti.github.io/docs/Scheckenhofer%20et%20al_2025-P&P.pdf)). Bank of Finland work uses mirror statistics and finds that growth came mainly from Central Asia and the Caucasus, but that their share of Russian imports is "still moderate" ([EconStor record of BOFIT PB 15/2023](https://www.econstor.eu/handle/10419/280400)). The BOFIT PDFs were not readable in this research, so these points come from summaries only.

### Mirror gaps: goods "lost in transit"

The October 2023 version of the EBRD paper adds a mirror test. It divides the imports that Armenia, Kyrgyzstan and Georgia report from each EU member and the UK by the exports that the EU side reports, at HS6. It sums March–December of each year, to absorb transit time ([Oxford-hosted Oct 2023 version](https://users.ox.ac.uk/~econ0247/Roundabout.pdf)). It excludes Kazakhstan, because detailed bilateral import data were not available. Normal CIF/FOB differences make this ratio usually above 1. A fall in the ratio for sanctioned goods means goods that the EU declares to a partner do not arrive in that partner's records. For Armenia and Kyrgyzstan, the ratio for sanctioned goods **fell about 50 percentage points in 2022 (about USD 0.8 bn "missing")**, against about 15 points for other goods. Vehicle parts (870830) went from 89–110% to 30%. At aggregate level, the **EU–Kazakhstan ratio went from 92% (2021) to 74% (2022), about USD 2 bn "lost in transit"** (same source). The published 2026 version says that rerouting plus lost-in-transit together offset about **one third** of the fall in European exports to Russia, with the two channels about equal ([RePEc abstract, EER 2026](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eecrev/v187y2026ics001429212600084x.html)).

### Russian transaction-level customs data

KSE used **transaction-level Russian import records**, validated against mirror data. It attributed each shipment in four steps: producer headquarters, country of manufacture, seller and dispatch country ([KSE Jan 2024](https://sanctions.kse.ua/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Challenges-of-Export-Controls-Enforcement.pdf)). This method shows "Western brand shipped via a third country", which HS6 mirror data cannot show. In Jan–Oct 2023, coalition-headquartered firms made **at least 43.9%** of Russia's battlefield-goods imports. By seller country, China had 38.0% and Hong Kong 30.9%, and **Kyrgyz sellers had USD 98 m (1.1%)** (same source). Kiel Report No. 9 uses complete Russian customs records to end-2023. It splits sanctioned imports into "rerouting" (made in a sanctioning country, shipped from another country) and "substitution" (made in the shipping country), with neutral exporters and non-sanctioned goods as controls. China accounts for **63% of estimated rerouting** of critical products ([Kiel Report No. 9](https://www.kielinstitut.de/fileadmin/Dateiverwaltung/IfW-Publications/fis-import/3d87c36f-c2f7-46a6-aedb-8c03627a224a-Endgame_The-State-of-the-Russian-Economy-Kiel_Report_No_9-english-Version.pdf)). Journalists use the same data type. Verstka used confidential Russian customs data ([Verstka](https://verstka.media/en/a-loophole-in-the-sanctions-sanctioned-goods-of-almost-all-types-worth-billions-of-dollars-are-imported-into-russia)), and RFE/RL Schemes traced 126 shipments of Kongsberg truck parts through a Turkish intermediary ([OilPrice reprint of Schemes](https://oilprice.com/Geopolitics/International/Leaked-Records-Reveal-Sanctioned-Truck-Parts-in-Russia.html)). The weak point is coverage after 2023. The Insider reports that about **180 sanctioned categories disappeared from Russian customs data in 2025** ([The Insider](https://theins.press/en/inv/288081)).

### Partner-reported CHPL-only trackers

Because transaction data are incomplete after 2023, KSE moved to **partner-reported exports to Russia in Comtrade at HS6**, plus monthly China customs data. Its quarterly tracker uses the 50-code list in six tiers (1, 2, 3.A, 3.B, 4.A, 4.B). The reporters cover about 97–98% of Russia's imports ([KSE CHP Tracker Q1 2026](https://institute.kse.ua/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/russian_chp_tracker_eng_q1_2026.pdf)). It finds that Russia's CHP imports fell by more than half in value and that China has about 75% of reported CHP exports to Russia (same source). Carnegie, with China customs data, finds that China's share of Russia's high-priority imports went from **32% (2021) to 89% (2023)**, with Armenia at 1.2% ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/russia-eurasia/politika/2024/05/behind-the-scenes-chinas-increasing-role-in-russias-defense-industry?lang=en)). S&P Global ran a series of CHPI analyses in 2023–2024 ([S&P Dec 2024](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/research/2024/12/fishing-for-chpis-trade-flows-adapt-to-sanctions-on-russia)).

### Governments use the same evidence, but publish few numbers

Governments publish lists and enforcement actions, not datasets. The CHPL grew from **9 HS codes (FinCEN–BIS alert, 19 May 2023)** to 45 codes in four tiers (September 2023) to **50 codes (22–23 February 2024)**. No newer version was found ([BIS CHPL](https://www.bis.gov/licensing/country-guidance/common-high-priority-items-list-chpl); [FinCEN](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/supplemental-alert-fincen-and-us-department-commerces-bureau-industry-and); [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/russia-sanctions-common-high-priority-items-list)). The EU created the Art. 12f anti-circumvention tool in June 2023. It used the tool for the first time on **23 April 2026**, against Kyrgyzstan, for CN 8457 10 (machining centres) and CN 8517 62 (data-transmission apparatus) ([Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/ac68233a/beyond-the-no-russia-clause-kyrgyzstan-becomes-the-first-test)). The Council based the decision on EU exports of these items to Kyrgyzstan compared with pre-war levels ([Steptoe](https://www.steptoe.com/en/news-publications/international-compliance-blog/eu-council-adopts-20th-sanctions-package-against-russia.html)). This is the same type of evidence that our project shows. The EU sanctions envoy gave Kyrgyz figures in Bishkek on 26 February 2026: EU exports of these items to Kyrgyzstan **+800%** and Kyrgyz exports of the same goods to Russia **+1,200%**, Jan–Oct 2025 against pre-invasion levels. This figure comes only from a search snippet of an RFE/RL report ([GlobalSecurity/RFE-RL snippet](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2026/02/mil-260226-rferl02.htm)). In Kazakhstan, the envoy said in June 2024 that high-priority imports showed "a significant decline", but he gave no numbers ([Interfax](https://interfax.com/newsroom/top-stories/103483/)). Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan respond with licensing, company closures and bank rules. They do not publish CHPL-specific statistics ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/kyrgyzstan-orders-50-companies-to-cease-activity-over-sanctions-risks/); [Baker McKenzie](https://sanctionsnews.bakermckenzie.com/kazakhstan-changes-regarding-monitoring-of-goods-re-exported-to-russia/)).

## Our numbers pass the external checks, but they are a lower bound

Our pipeline produces results that match other sources in direction and, where the scope is the same, in size. This is good evidence that the data pull is correct. The checks also show where our numbers are low.

| Check | External figure | Our figure | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| KZ→RU 2022 vs 2021 | +401% ([Moscow Times, Comtrade](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2024/09/16/should-the-west-sanction-russias-neighbors-in-central-asia-a86310/pdf)) | +390% (62.5 → 306.0) | Match |
| KZ→RU 2023 vs 2022 | −5%, about USD 300 m (same source) | −4.3%, USD 292.7 m | Match |
| EU→KG, Jan–Oct 2025 vs pre-war | +800% (×9), snippet only | 2025 vs 2019–21 avg: ×4.7; vs 2021: ×6.6 | Ours lower |
| KG→RU, Jan–Oct 2025 vs pre-war | +1,200% (×13), snippet only | 2025 vs 2019–21 avg: ×10.8; vs 2021: ×8.5 | Ours lower |
| KG→RU 2023 | USD 98 m by Kyrgyz sellers, Jan–Oct, Russian customs ([KSE](https://sanctions.kse.ua/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Challenges-of-Export-Controls-Enforcement.pdf)) | USD 31.3 m, full year, Kyrgyz-reported | Ours ≈ one third |
| KG+UZ+AM→RU 2023 | about USD 11.6 m, exporter-reported ([S&P](https://www.tradefinanceglobal.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/SPGMI2264_Whitepaper_Transshipment-hubs-facilitating-goods-to-Russia.pdf)) | KG alone USD 31.3 m | S&P lower; scope unclear |

The Kyrgyz multiples differ from the EU figures for known reasons. The EU compares ten months of 2025 with an unknown "pre-invasion" base. We compare full years. A small base makes the multiple very sensitive: our 2019–21 average for KG→RU is only **USD 1.5 m**. The EU can use CN8 lines or a subset of "these items". The currency also changes the result. In EUR terms, our EU→KG 2025/2021 multiple is about ×6.9, not ×6.6, because the ECB rate moved from 1.18 to 1.13 USD per EUR. Our project uses the full-year 49-code basket. Thus our lower multiples are not an error, but the report and the site must say which baseline and which list each multiple uses.

The KSE comparison is the strongest illustration of under-reporting. Russian customs records show **USD 98 m of battlefield goods sold by Kyrgyz sellers in only ten months of 2023**. Kyrgyzstan reports **USD 31.3 m for the full year**. The two figures do not measure the same thing. KSE counts seller country and used the 45-code list of that time. We count the Kyrgyz reporter's exports of 49 codes. But the size of the gap agrees with the IMF view that intra-EAEU trade has no mandatory customs declarations, and that unrecorded re-exports are about **30% of Kyrgyz total imports**, mostly to Russia ([IMF Country Report 2024/065, snippet](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2024/065/article-A002-en.xml)). Kazakhstan compiles EAEU trade from statistical form 1-TS, not from customs declarations ([stat.gov.kz](https://stat.gov.kz/api/iblock/element/451974/file/en/)). EBRD and KSE both warn that EAEU records are incomplete ([EBRD WP 276](https://www.ebrd.com/content/dam/ebrd_dxp/assets/pdfs/office-of-the-chief-economist/working-papers/working-papers-2023/WP-276.pdf); [KSE Jan 2024](https://sanctions.kse.ua/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Challenges-of-Export-Controls-Enforcement.pdf)). Our second leg is therefore "recorded re-export", a lower bound. The S&P figure is lower than ours, although it covers three countries. S&P does not state its list version, so this difference needs a check before we use it.

Our tier data show a second limit. For Kazakhstan, Tier 3.B (bearings and related goods) exports to Russia are larger than EU Tier 3.B exports to Kazakhstan in every year. In 2024 they were **USD 139.2 m against USD 57.0 m** (our `trade_by_code.csv`). Thus the EU is not the main source for this tier. KSE names Kazakhstan as a Tier 3.B diversion route ([KSE CHP Tracker Q1 2026](https://institute.kse.ua/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/russian_chp_tracker_eng_q1_2026.pdf)), and Chinese exports of 45 dual-use goods to Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan rose 64% in 2023 to USD 1.3 bn ([Caspian Policy Center](https://caspianpolicy.org/research/security/central-asias-gateway-of-dual-use-technology-and-materials-to-russia)). Our "exports to Russia ÷ EU exports" ratio is therefore not a re-export share. It mixes EU goods, Chinese goods and local goods. The site must label it as a scale ratio only.

## Known pitfalls cluster in five places

Five types of pitfall appear again and again in the notes. Our pipeline already handles some of them. It does not handle others.

**Data availability and lags.** Russia's customs service stopped product-level publication in spring 2022 and resumed only headline totals in March 2023 ([Moscow Times](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2023/03/13/russia-resumes-publication-of-trade-statistics-after-year-long-pause-a80467)). KSE reports that the UAE gives no product-level data and that Kazakhstan's latest Comtrade data end in **February 2024** ([KSE CHP Tracker Q1 2026](https://institute.kse.ua/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/russian_chp_tracker_eng_q1_2026.pdf)). Our pipeline shows that the Comtrade public preview does return Kazakhstan's and Kyrgyzstan's annual exports to Russia for 2024 and 2025. The KSE statement therefore probably refers to monthly data or to import data. We must check monthly availability before we add a monthly Comtrade leg. China had not sent 2025 data to Comtrade at the time of the Q1 2026 KSE edition (same source). Comtrade+ shows availability dashboards and "Revised" marks ([comtradeplus.un.org](https://comtradeplus.un.org/)). A risk in our own code: `build.py` fills missing years with zero (`fillna({"usd": 0.0})`). KSE and Bruegel both treat missing as missing and state any zero assumption ([Bruegel](https://www.bruegel.org/dataset/russian-foreign-trade-tracker)).

**API limits and breakdown rows.** The free preview gives at most **500 records per call**. A free Basic key gives the Data API with up to 100,000 records per call ([Comtrade wiki](https://comtradewiki.azurewebsites.net/?p=78)). Our largest call returns 47 records, so the cap is not a problem now. But it truncates without a warning, and a wider query (more partners, monthly, control goods) will hit it. Comtrade+ can also return breakdowns by transport mode, customs procedure and second partner. Our current raw files contain only total rows (`motCode` 0, `customsCode` C00, `partner2Code` 0), so there is no double count now. But `build.py` sums all rows without a filter, so a future pull can double count without a warning.

**Nomenclature changes.** The WCO HS2017–HS2022 tables give no full correlation for some new Chapter 85 subheadings ([WCO](https://www.wcoomd.org/en/topics/nomenclature/instrument-and-tools/hs-nomenclature-2022-edition/correlation-tables-hs-2017-2022.aspx)). Our handling of 854140→854149 (excluded from the basket) is more careful than most trackers, which do not state their concordance at all. Our raw files show that both Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan switched from H5 (HS2017) to H6 (HS2022) in 2022. The CHPL itself changed from 45 to 50 codes, so comparisons with 2023 studies need a list-version note ([KSE Jan 2024](https://sanctions.kse.ua/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Challenges-of-Export-Controls-Enforcement.pdf); [BIS CHPL](https://www.bis.gov/licensing/country-guidance/common-high-priority-items-list-chpl)).

**HS6 is wider than the control.** Sanctions often cover only some HS8 lines inside an HS6 code. EBRD shows the misclassification risk with x-ray apparatus: medical 902213 is not sanctioned, 902219 is sanctioned ([EBRD WP 276](https://www.ebrd.com/content/dam/ebrd_dxp/assets/pdfs/office-of-the-chief-economist/working-papers/working-papers-2023/WP-276.pdf)). Comext has CN8 detail, which can narrow the EU leg. Comext also suppresses some confidential values ([Eurostat metadata](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/env_trdrrm_esms.htm)).

**Growth is not proof.** KSE says that rising coalition CHP exports to EAEU members "should not necessarily serve as evidence" of circumvention ([KSE CHP Tracker Q1 2026](https://institute.kse.ua/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/russian_chp_tracker_eng_q1_2026.pdf)). Kazakhstan's EU imports rose for normal reasons too: Kazakh imports from the EU and UK rose 82.8% in early 2023, and imports from non-EU, non-EAEU countries rose 72.6% ([OECD 2024](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/06/diversifying-kazakhstan-s-exports_6db7322e/7f9cd8aa-en.pdf)). Bloomberg economists linked only 7% of Kazakhstan's 2023 export growth to Russia to sanctioned goods ([bne IntelliNews](https://www.intellinews.com/rerouting-of-russia-trade-to-add-more-caucasus-central-asia-growth-in-2024-2025-says-oxford-economics-330766/)). Values can also overstate growth through prices ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/transshipments-from-the-eu-to-russia)). Only control groups or a mirror gap can separate circumvention from normal growth. Our project has neither yet.

## Burana is the closest rival, and nobody owns the open niche

The tracker landscape has four groups: aggregate mirror-trade trackers, CHPL reports in PDF, sanctions-list databases and one-off charts. No public product combines CHPL detail, third-country corridors, an open pipeline, an interactive view and a regular update.

| Product | Scope | Method | Format and cadence | Open data/code |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bruegel Russian foreign trade tracker | 38 partners, ~80% of Russian trade; aggregate/SITC, not CHPL | Partner mirror data, monthly | Excel, monthly; DOI citation; per-source cutoffs; updated 6 Nov 2026 ([Bruegel](https://www.bruegel.org/dataset/russian-foreign-trade-tracker)) | Excel download; no licence found |
| KSE CHP Imports Tracker | 50 CHP codes, six tiers, all partners | Partner-reported Comtrade + China/Taiwan customs | PDF, quarterly from April 2026 ([KSE](https://sanctions.kse.ua/en/export-controls-have-reduced-but-not-eliminated-russia-s-access-to-war-critical-technology/)) | No dataset |
| Burana / Corridor Watch | EU→KZ, KG, AM; Georgia as control; 50 CHPL codes in 4 tiers | EU exports vs partner imports against 2019–21 baseline; "unaccounted share"; reports a failed control check | Web page; Eurostat data of 2026-07-16; no stated cadence ([Burana](https://burana.vercel.app)) | Repo exists ([GitHub](https://github.com/colton-hong/burana)); no licence or downloads confirmed |
| Silverado dashboards | Russian imports, chips, robots | Mirror export data | Static images; dated Jan 2024 ([Silverado](https://www.silverado.org/data-dashboards/monthly-russian-goods-imports-dashboard)) | No |
| CREA Russia Fossil Tracker | Fossil fuels (design reference) | Kpler, ENTSOG, Eurostat | Daily; methodology page; dated change notices with effect size (−7.0%) ([CREA](https://energyandcleanair.org/russia-fossil-tracker-updated-methodology/)) | Code repo indexed |
| Brookings (Robin Brooks) | DE, IT, JP, LT, PL, UK, US → KG, KZ, GE, AM | IMF DOTS; exporter vs importer pairs for false invoicing | 8 static charts, one-off ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/transshipments-from-the-eu-to-russia)) | No |
| Our project | EU→KZ, KG and KZ, KG→RU; 49 CHPL codes, six tiers | Two-leg descriptive, 2019–2025 annual | Static site with interactive charts; no stated cadence | Code and processed CSV in repo; no licence, citation or changelog yet |

Burana is ahead of us on method. It has a mirror gap, a control country and Armenia, and it publishes its failed checks. We are ahead of Burana on the second leg (exports to Russia), on the six-tier detail and on code-level views. We are also ahead on open data: our processed CSV files are in the repository. A GitHub search for "common high priority", "chpl russia" and "russia re-export comtrade" found no repositories (`gh search repos`, 18 Nov 2026, per the research notes). Silverado and Castellum dashboards stopped around 2024 ([Castellum](https://www.castellum.ai/russia-sanctions-dashboard)), so regular maintenance is itself a differentiator. Our best position is "open, reproducible and maintained", not "most complete". KSE and S&P have wider data. Transaction-data studies see brands and sellers that no aggregate tracker can see.

## Conclusion

The research changes what our project should claim. The two rising lines do not show re-export on their own. The second leg is survey-based, and the IMF and KSE evidence says it captures perhaps one third or less of the Kyrgyz flow. The first leg mixes normal post-2022 import growth with diversion. Non-EU sources also feed the second leg, so the ratio between the lines is not a re-export share. The value of the project lies in three things that no public tracker gives together: an open, code-level pipeline; the second leg into Russia, which Burana does not show; and a fast, documented update. Items 1–10 make the current figures honest and citable in about a week. Items 11–13 then add the mirror gap and control groups. With them, the project can answer the question that KSE and the EU leave open: whether the Central Asian corridor still carries excess CHPL trade after 2024.

One open question deserves direct attention. The EU's +800% and +1,200% Kyrgyz figures are larger than ours. They come from a search snippet, with an unknown baseline and an unknown code list. If a mirror check and a CN8 pull can reproduce or correct them, the project will deliver its most useful single result: an independent, reproducible test of the evidence behind the first use of the EU anti-circumvention tool.
