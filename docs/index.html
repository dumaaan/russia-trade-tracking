The Central Asian detour

Battlefield goods are the 50 types of electronics, bearings and machine tools that Western governments found in Russian weapons in Ukraine. This page follows them on one route: exported by the EU to Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, then exported by those two countries to Russia.

Both legs, year by year

    Show the numbers

    Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan compared

    Kazakhstan's flows are six to ten times larger. Kyrgyzstan's grew much faster, from a very small base.

    The ratio is not a re-export share. The EU is not the only supplier: China and other countries also sell these goods to Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. Kazakhstan's Tier 3.B exports to Russia (bearings, optics) are larger than the EU's Tier 3.B exports to Kazakhstan in every year.

    Show the numbers

    Does the EU shipment arrive?

    Each line divides what the country records as imports from the EU by what the EU records as exports to it. Below 100%, part of the shipments is missing from the country's records.

    Some gap is normal: goods arrive in another year, importers record the country of origin, and import values include freight. So each year is compared with the country's own pre-war ratio. The excess gap is the part above that level. Kazakhstan gives no product detail for imports by partner for 2020–2022, so its pre-war ratio uses 2019 only.

    Show the numbers

    Did the April 2026 ban work?

    On 23 April 2026 the EU used its anti-circumvention tool for the first time: it banned exports to Kyrgyzstan of machining centres (8457.10) and data transmission equipment (8517.62). Each column is one month. The dark line over each window is its monthly average: April 2025 to March 2026 before the ban, May 2026 onwards after it. April 2026 is left out. Each chart has its own scale.

    Read with care: five months of EU data and four of Kyrgyz data, both provisional. Code 8517.62 also covers ordinary routers and switches. A rise in Kyrgyzstan's exports to Russia after the ban means that the goods came from somewhere else, for example from China or from stock.

    Data and method

    • trade_totals.csv totals by flow, country and year, in USD and EUR
    • trade_by_code.csv values by HS code and tier
    • comparison.csv indexes, ratios and mirror gaps
    • availability.csv which source has which year
    • ban_monthly.csv monthly series for the April 2026 ban
    • chpl_hs_codes.csv the list of codes and tiers

    Method

    • The list. The EU version of February 2024 of the Common High Priority List: 50 HS codes in four tiers. Earlier versions had 9 codes (May 2023) and 45 codes (September 2023), so totals from 2023 studies are not directly comparable.
    • 49 codes, not 50. Code 854149 is left out of the totals. Its 2017 code, 854140, also holds solar cells and LEDs, which would make 2019–2021 much too high.
    • EU exports. Eurostat Comext, dataset DS-045409, EU-27, in EUR. USD values use the ECB annual average rate.
    • Exports to Russia and imports from the EU. UN Comtrade, as reported by Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. Russia stopped publishing product-level trade data after 2021.
    • HS editions. 2019–2021 data uses HS 2017. Codes 880730 and 852589 use their 2017 codes, 880330 and 852580, in those years.
    • Baseline. The 2019–2021 average, because one year can be unusual and small values make a one-year base unstable. 2021 alone is the second option.
    • Missing data. A year that the source does not report is missing, not zero.
    • Limits. Exports to Russia are probably under-reported, because trade inside the Eurasian Economic Union has no customs declarations. An HS code is wider than the sanctioned goods. Two rising lines can show re-export, but they do not prove it.
