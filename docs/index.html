The Central Asian detour

Western governments have found 50 kinds of electronics, bearings and machine tools inside Russian weapons in Ukraine. This site follows those battlefield goods on a detour: out of the EU and China, into Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan, and from there into Russia. It tracks 49 of the 50 customs codes; light sensors have no comparable data before 2022.

    Part 2 How reliable the records are

    Goods leave the EU and vanish from the books

    When goods leave the EU, the EU counts them. When they arrive, the importer should count them too. These lines show how much of the supplier's tally turns up on the other side: blue for the EU, black for China. Below 100%, goods have gone missing from the books.

    Some slippage is normal: a December shipment lands in January, importers file goods by country of origin, and import prices include freight. So each country is measured against its own pre-war record, and only the gap beyond that counts. Kazakhstan's pre-war record is 2019 alone, because it published no import detail by partner for 2020–2022.

    What went missing

    Kazakhstan's data for 2020–2022 are missing for both suppliers. Chinese goods shipped through Hong Kong are filed as imports from China, which can push the China line above 100%. The China line always covers all 49 codes; the goods filter changes only the EU line.

    Part 3 Tests

    Battlefield goods as a share of all trade

    If Central Asia were simply trading more with everyone, battlefield goods would hold a steady share of the total. These lines track that share: EU sales to each country on the left, the country's sales to Russia on the right.

    A flat line means the goods kept pace with trade as a whole. A rising one means they pulled ahead. Every small line in the table shares one scale.

    Battlefield goods against their closest relatives

    A tougher test: compare battlefield goods with their closest relatives, the rest of the machines (84), electronics (85) and instruments (90) in the customs book. Each line divides the growth of battlefield goods by the growth of the rest: above ×1, battlefield goods were growing faster.

    Two caveats. Many of the comparison goods are also banned for export to Russia, so this pits priority goods against other technical goods, not banned goods against legal ones. And three countries are too few to prove that the customs union is the cause. Bearings (8482) were the bulk of Kazakhstan's pre-war sales to Russia, so every result appears with and without them. Georgia, Azerbaijan and Mongolia sell too little to Russia to test and show only the EU side. Aircraft (chapter 88) are left out: one plane can be worth $100–500M.

    What a kilogram costs

    Price works like a fingerprint. For each customs code, compare what a kilogram costs from the EU, from China and on its way to Russia. If the Russia price sits near the EU's, the goods look European; near China's, they look Chinese or otherwise cheap.

    A fingerprint, not proof: one code can cover cheap and expensive products. Hollow dots mark weights the reporting country estimated. Uzbekistan estimates or skips most weights, so it has few rows or none. Codes under $100k a year to Russia are not shown.

    Part 4 Enforcement

    Did enforcement change trade?

    The line is each country's monthly sales of battlefield goods to Russia. Under it, each mark is one step: a US, UK or EU blacklisting or sanctions package, or something the country's own government or banks did or said. Hover over a mark to see what it was.

    The test behind the headline

    For each step aimed at one country, we compare its sales in the six months after with the six before, against the same change for the other two countries, and set that against the usual range in months without a blacklisting. Steps close together share months, so a cluster of steps is one fall, not several. Steps that hit all three countries at once, steps before September 2022 and steps less than three months old cannot be tested this way. A fall after a step does not prove the step caused it.

    What the governments said and did

    Every row is something a government, central bank or bank in Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan or Uzbekistan said or did about sanctions; each one is a mark on the "own government, banks" row of the chart that opens Part 4. The last column is the test behind that chart: sales in the six months that followed against the six before, net of the other two countries.

    The sources are news reports of official statements; one measure rests only on Ukrainian intelligence and is marked as such. The record is a sample, not a full list, so "first" means first in this record. A move in sales after a statement does not mean the statement caused it.

    Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan · from January 2022 · listed exports to Russia in current US dollars, each row on its own scale

    EU cases that name the route

    Each mark is one step in a case where EU authorities say goods went to Russia through Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan or Uzbekistan. Open marks are investigations and arrests, grey marks are charges, and solid marks are convictions and fines. A line joins the steps of one case; the number leads to the table.

    This is a sample, not a full count. Most investigations stay private, and not every judgment is published. The table says for each step whether our source is the authority's or court's own page or a news report or law-firm summary. When we could not find the date of the event, the mark sits at the first report. We left out goods that only claimed a Central Asian origin, and goods bound for Belarus.

    What blacklisting did to trade

    When a country gets a batch of new listings in one month, call it a wave. Each wave is the first row of its country in the chart that opens Part 4; the table below sums them up.

    Payment channels, judged by name, are banks, payment and processing firms, brokers, crypto exchanges and crypto or stablecoin issuers. Everything else counts as trading and other firms. Sber Vostok (Kazakhstan) is a payment channel on its Sberbank-group name alone. One payment channel makes a wave a payment-channel wave.

    Sanctions events, 2023–2026

    Each sanctions event gets the same test: average monthly sales over the six months after it, against the six before, skipping the month itself. Then three checks: did battlefield goods fall, did trade slip into neighbouring codes under the same HS4 heading, and did it move to other countries.

    An event aimed at one country is measured against the other two. One that hit all three is measured against EU sales to Türkiye, the UAE, Georgia and Azerbaijan. Flows under $50k a month beforehand are shown as dollar changes. Plenty else happens in the same months, so a fall after an event is not proof the event caused it. Kazakhstan's 2024 figures come from its statistics bureau; Comtrade has only January and February. Events aimed at one country also appear in the chart that opens Part 4, with the same test as every other step.

    Part 5 How much reached Russia

    How much reached Russia through each country

    There are two ways to answer, and they are kept apart. The first counts goods that physically passed through each country. The low end is what the country reports exporting to Russia. The high end adds every EU and Chinese shipment that arrived but vanished from the import books, beyond the usual pre-war gap: if all of it went on to Russia, that is the ceiling. The second counts what the country's companies sold, using Russia's own customs records by seller, which include goods shipped from elsewhere.

    Code explorer

    Everything here is for one code, in current US dollars, with the filters switched off. China's and Hong Kong's exports are their own figures; recorded imports are the route country's. When recorded imports drift far from their pre-war ratio to the supplier's exports, goods are either vanishing from the importer's books or being filed under another code or origin.
