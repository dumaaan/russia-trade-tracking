The Central Asian detour

Western governments have found 50 kinds of electronics, bearings and machine tools inside Russian weapons in Ukraine. This site follows those battlefield goods on a detour: out of the EU and China, into Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan, and from there into Russia. It tracks 49 of the 50 customs codes; light sensors have no comparable data before 2022.

    Part 2 How reliable the records are

    Goods leave the EU and vanish from the books

    When goods leave the EU, the EU counts them. When they arrive, the importer should count them too. These lines show how much of the EU's tally turns up on the other side. Below 100%, goods have gone missing from the books.

    Some slippage is normal: a December shipment lands in January, importers file goods by country of origin, and import prices include freight. So each country is measured against its own pre-war record, and only the gap beyond that counts. Kazakhstan's pre-war record is 2019 alone, because it published no import detail by partner for 2020–2022.

    Battlefield goods against their closest relatives

    A tougher test: compare battlefield goods with their closest relatives, the rest of the machines (84), electronics (85) and instruments (90) in the customs book. Where a coloured line climbs above the grey one, battlefield goods were growing faster.

    Two caveats. Many of the comparison goods are also banned for export to Russia, so this pits priority goods against other technical goods, not banned goods against legal ones. And three countries are too few to prove that the customs union is the cause. Bearings (8482) were the bulk of Kazakhstan's pre-war sales to Russia, so every result appears with and without them. Georgia, Azerbaijan and Mongolia sell too little to Russia to test and show only the EU side. Aircraft (chapter 88) are left out: one plane can be worth $100–500M.

    What a kilogram costs

    Price works like a fingerprint. For each customs code, compare what a kilogram costs from the EU, from China and on its way to Russia. If the Russia price sits near the EU's, the goods look European; near China's, they look Chinese or otherwise cheap.

    A fingerprint, not proof: one code can cover cheap and expensive products. Hollow dots mark weights the reporting country estimated. Uzbekistan estimates or skips most weights, so it has few rows or none. Codes under $100k a year to Russia are not shown.

    Part 4 Enforcement

    What the governments said and did

    Every numbered mark is something a government, central bank or bank in Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan or Uzbekistan said or did about sanctions. It sits on the line of that country's monthly sales of battlefield goods to Russia. The table gives the source and how sales moved in the six months that followed, compared with the six before, where at least three months of data exist.

    Marks fall into five kinds: promises not to become a route around sanctions, steps the country took itself, steps by its banks, statements playing the sanctions down, and objections to them. The sources are news reports of official statements; one measure rests only on Ukrainian intelligence and is marked as such. The record is a sample, not a full list, so "first" means first in this record. Marks are numbered country by country. A move in sales after a statement does not mean the statement caused it.

    What blacklisting did to trade

    When a country gets a batch of new listings in one month, call it a wave. Each coloured dot shows what came next: the change in the country's sales to Russia over the following six months, compared with the six before and net of its two neighbours. The grey ticks run the same test on every other month. A dot far to the left of the ticks is a fall out of the ordinary.

    Payment channels, judged by name, are banks, payment and processing firms, brokers, crypto exchanges and crypto or stablecoin issuers. Everything else counts as trading and other firms. Sber Vostok (Kazakhstan) is a payment channel on its Sberbank-group name alone. One payment channel makes a wave a payment-channel wave.

    Sanctions events, 2023–2026

    Each sanctions event gets the same test: average monthly sales over the six months after it, against the six before, skipping the month itself. Then three checks: did battlefield goods fall, did trade slip into neighbouring codes under the same HS4 heading, and did it move to other countries.

    An event aimed at one country is measured against the other two. One that hit all three is measured against EU sales to Türkiye, the UAE, Georgia and Azerbaijan. Flows under $50k a month beforehand are shown as dollar changes. Plenty else happens in the same months, so a fall after an event is not proof the event caused it. Kazakhstan's 2024 figures come from its statistics bureau; Comtrade has only January and February.

    Part 5 How much reached Russia

    How much reached Russia through each country

    There are two ways to answer, and they are kept apart. The first counts goods that physically passed through each country. The low end is what the country reports exporting to Russia. The high end adds every EU and Chinese shipment that arrived but vanished from the import books, beyond the usual pre-war gap: if all of it went on to Russia, that is the ceiling. The second counts what the country's companies sold, using Russia's own customs records by seller, which include goods shipped from elsewhere.

    Part 6 One code, the whole route

    Code explorer

    Everything here is for one code, in current US dollars, with the filters switched off. China's and Hong Kong's exports are their own figures; recorded imports are the route country's. When recorded imports drift far from their pre-war ratio to the supplier's exports, goods are either vanishing from the importer's books or being filed under another code or origin.

    Recorded imports against the supplier's exports

    At single-code level the two sides often file the same goods differently: chips can land under 8542.31, 8542.39 or 8471.
